# Spec by Example — Ten Stuck Things

> Each example: the stuck thought as a person would actually type it, what's
> really going on, what the app should ask (if anything), and the ideal tiny
> next action. These double as prompt-engineering data and eyeball test cases.
>
> Reminder of the bar for a valid action: physical, 5–15 minutes, singular,
> concrete enough that a stranger could verify it happened.

---

## 1. The avoided paperwork

**Stuck thought:** "I need to deal with my taxes."

**What's really going on:** Fog + dread. "Taxes" is a project wearing a task
costume. The person doesn't know what step one even is, which reads as dread.

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Find last year's tax return PDF and drag it into a new
folder on your desktop called `taxes-2026`. That's it — don't open it."

**If "still too big":** "Open your email and search `tax return`. Don't read
anything, just confirm the search returns results."

---

## 2. The shameful room

**Stuck thought:** "The garage is a disaster, I keep meaning to do something
about it."

**What's really going on:** The size of the mess makes any start feel
pointless. Perfectionism disguised as procrastination.

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Set a 10-minute timer, grab one trash bag, and fill it
with only the obvious garbage. When the timer rings, stop — even mid-bag."

**Key principle shown:** The stop condition matters as much as the start.
Permission to stop is what makes starting safe.

---

## 3. The identity goal

**Stuck thought:** "I should really get back into exercising."

**What's really going on:** "Get back into" is an identity change, not an
action. Identity changes can't be scheduled; first sessions can.

**Clarifying questions:** One, with a default: "What did you used to enjoy —
walking, gym, something else? (If unsure, we'll go with walking.)"

**Tiny action:** "Tonight, put your shoes next to the front door. Tomorrow,
walk to the end of your street and back. Not a workout — a rehearsal."

---

## 4. The life fog

**Stuck thought:** "I hate my job and have to figure out my career."

**What's really going on:** Too big for any app to "solve" — and it
shouldn't try. The goal is to convert rumination into a small piece of
evidence.

**Clarifying questions:** None. Asking "what do you want to do with your
life?" IS the fog. Never reflect the fog back at the user.

**Tiny action:** "Write three sentences about the last time work felt good —
what you were doing, who was around, what made it good. Save it anywhere."

**Key principle shown:** For life-sized problems, the tiny action gathers
data about the person, it doesn't pick a direction.

---

## 5. The dreaded phone call

**Stuck thought:** "I need to make a doctor's appointment but keep putting
it off."

**What's really going on:** Phone-call avoidance. "Make the appointment" is
actually three actions: find the number, decide what to say, dial.

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Find the office's phone number and save it as a contact.
You are explicitly NOT calling today."

**Key principle shown:** Splitting off the scary part and forbidding it
today removes the reason to avoid the easy part.

---

## 6. The money fog

**Stuck thought:** "My finances are a mess."

**What's really going on:** Fear of a number. The mess is unmeasured, so
imagination fills in the worst case.

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Log into your main bank account and write down one number
on a sticky note: the current checking balance. No judging, no scrolling
through transactions. One number."

---

## 7. The ambition fog

**Stuck thought:** "I want to build an app but I don't know where to start."

**What's really going on:** The gap between the imagined finished thing and
the blank page. Tutorial-shopping and stack-debating are overthinking in a
developer costume.

**Clarifying questions:** One: "What should the app do, in one sentence? A
rough answer is fine."

**Tiny action:** "Write 5 example inputs a user would give your app and the
ideal response for each, in a plain text file. That file is your spec."

---

## 8. The relationship weight

**Stuck thought:** "I haven't talked to my dad in months and it's weighing
on me."

**What's really going on:** Guilt compounds: the longer the silence, the
bigger the imagined required gesture. The debt feels like it needs a big
payment; it needs any payment.

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Send one text: 'Saw something today that made me think of
you. How've you been?' Don't plan the conversation after it."

---

## 9. The ignored warning sign

**Stuck thought:** "The car is making a noise and I keep ignoring it."

**What's really going on:** Fear of the cost, so the problem stays
unmeasured (see #6 — same mechanism, different domain).

**Clarifying questions:** None needed.

**Tiny action:** "Next time you drive, have your phone record 30 seconds of
the noise. That recording is the whole job for today — not the mechanic,
not the estimate."

---

## 10. The vague virtue

**Stuck thought:** "I want to eat better."

**What's really going on:** A virtue, not a plan. Virtues fail at decision
time (6pm, tired, hungry) because nothing was decided in advance.

**Clarifying questions:** One, with a default: "Which meal goes worst right
now? (If unsure: breakfast — it's the most automatable.)"

**Tiny action:** "Tonight, write tomorrow's breakfast on a sticky note and
put it on the coffee maker. One meal, decided once, in advance."

---

## Patterns the Examples Encode

1. **Never reflect the fog back.** If the user says "figure out my life,"
   don't ask "what are your life goals?" — that's the same fog as a question.
2. **Zero or one clarifying question is the norm.** Two is the hard maximum,
   and every question ships with a default so "I don't know" still moves
   forward.
3. **Forbid the scary part explicitly** ("you are NOT calling today") —
   it makes the easy part safe to do.
4. **Stop conditions are part of the action** ("when the timer rings, stop").
5. **For unmeasured fears, the action is measurement** (one number, one
   recording), never a fix.
6. **For life-sized fog, the action gathers evidence about the person**,
   it never picks a direction.
